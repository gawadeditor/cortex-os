# Input Reference — EP 003

- **Trial identifier:** `ep_003`
- **Topic:** The Antikythera Mechanism: Who Taught the Greeks the Sky?
- **Central question:** How could the ancient Greeks build a device this complex when nothing like it existed before — and nothing like it would exist again for over a thousand years?
- **Central answer (must not be spoiled early):** The mechanism was not a sudden invention but the final product of a centuries-long Babylonian-Greek knowledge synthesis — a tradition that left no physical trace because it was transmitted through minds, not machines. The device is not the beginning of something; it is the last breath of a dying intellectual lineage.

## Narrative Arc (must be preserved)

- **Setup:** The Hook and Context sections establish the mechanism as an impossible object — the most complex ancient device ever found, with no precursor, no successor, and no named maker. The viewer is grounded in its physical reality (82 fragments, Koine Greek inscriptions, Hellenistic civic culture) so that the mystery is felt as a real historical problem, not a fantasy.
- **Escalation:** Rising Mystery and Escalation reveal that the knowledge inside the device is centuries older than the device itself, that Babylonian observational data was absorbed and reworked by Greek geometers, that Hipparchus's lunar theory is mechanised in the gears, and that Cicero described similar devices attributed to Archimedes. The viewer realises there was an entire tradition of such machines — and all of them are gone.
- **Revelation:** The Revelation section lands the central reframe: Archimedes likely founded the tradition in 3rd-century BCE Syracuse, making the mechanism the endpoint of a 150-year lineage, not a sudden invention. The engineering-flaw counterpoint (jamming, demonstration model) is resolved as reinforcing the device's nature as a monument to knowledge rather than a practical tool.
- **Resolution:** The Resolution elevates the mystery by confronting the viewer with the tradition's total extinction — geared astronomical devices vanished for over a millennium. The mechanism was being carried somewhere by sea when it was lost. The builder, the school, the destination, and the reason for the journey all remain unknown. The device is the last physical trace of a civilisation's way of knowing the sky.

## Section Structure (order and purpose must be preserved)

- **Hook** — Open with the impossibility. Present the mechanism as an object that should not exist — no precursor, no successor, no named maker. The viewer must feel the vertigo of the gap before any context is given. Establish the central question immediately: who taught the Greeks to build this? (tension: high)
- **Context** — Ground the viewer in the physical reality of the device: 82 fragments, only one-third surviving, recovered from a shipwreck dated to 70–60 BCE. Establish that the inscriptions are entirely in Koine Greek — this is not alien, not Atlantean, but firmly Hellenistic. Introduce the civic culture encoded in the dials (Olympiad cycle, festivals). This section loads the viewer with the 'what' so the 'how' becomes unbearable. (tension: medium)
- **Rising Mystery** — Reveal what the device actually does — it encodes the Saros and Metonic eclipse cycles, which required centuries of continuous astronomical observation. Introduce the first layer of strangeness: the knowledge inside the device is far older than the device itself. The eclipse dial anchors to 205 BCE, decades before the mechanism was physically built. The device's brain is older than its body. This section transforms the mechanism from a 'what' mystery into a 'when' mystery. (tension: high)
- **Escalation** — Compound the mystery with three escalating revelations. First: Greek astronomers absorbed Babylonian arithmetical period relations and reworked them into geometric models — a documented cross-cultural knowledge transfer spanning centuries. Second: the lunar gearing precisely matches Hipparchus-style epicyclic theory, linking the device to a specific astronomer's mind around 150–125 BCE. Third: Cicero described mechanical celestial models attributed to Archimedes, implying a wider tradition of such devices already existed. The viewer now understands there was an entire school of device-builders — and every single one of their machines is gone except this one. (tension: high)
- **Revelation** — Land the central reframing. Tony Freeth argues Archimedes probably started the tradition of geared astronomical devices in 3rd-century BCE Syracuse. The mechanism is not a miracle of sudden invention — it is the last surviving product of a tradition that ran for at least 150 years. Then introduce the ironic counterpoint: recent engineering simulations suggest the device may have been prone to jamming, possibly making it a demonstration model rather than a practical instrument. Resolve this contradiction: even if imperfect mechanically, the device perfectly encodes the astronomical knowledge — it was a monument to understanding, not a tool for daily use. The central answer lands: this is the endpoint of a dying tradition, not the beginning of one. (tension: high)
- **Resolution** — Elevate the mystery to its final form. The tradition of geared astronomical devices died out entirely after the Hellenistic period and did not reappear for over a millennium. The mechanism was being transported by sea when it was lost — we do not know where it was going, who commissioned the journey, or whether it was a gift, a sale, or war loot. The anonymous builder, the vanished school, the drowned ship — the mechanism is not just an artefact, it is the last physical trace of an entire way of knowing the sky. Close with the unanswered questions that remain: the Corinthian calendar hypothesis, the Saros dialect geography, the destination of the ship. The viewer leaves not with closure but with elevation. (tension: medium)

## Key Reveals (facts that must land correctly)

- Answers 'when was this knowledge created?' by showing the eclipse dial anchors to 205 BCE — decades before the device was built. Splits the device into two ages: the age of its body and the age of its brain. Opens the question of who computed the data. (type: supporting)
- Answers 'how did Babylonian data reach Greek engineers?' with documented cross-cultural knowledge transfer. Transforms the device from a Greek achievement into a Babylonian-Greek synthesis. This is the turning point that reframes the entire origin story. (type: supporting)
- Links the device to a specific astronomer's mind — Hipparchus. The gearing is not abstract engineering; it is one person's theory of the moon made physical. Personalises the anonymous tradition. (type: supporting)
- Cicero's testimony proves the mechanism was not unique — similar devices existed and were attributed to Archimedes. Answers 'was this a one-off?' with a definitive no, and opens the devastating follow-up: where are all the others? (type: supporting)
- The central reveal. Freeth's argument that Archimedes founded the tradition in 3rd-century BCE Syracuse answers the episode's central question: the device is the endpoint of a 150-year lineage, not a sudden invention. The 'who taught the Greeks the sky' question is answered: centuries of Babylonian observers, generations of Greek geometers, and a school of device-builders that left no trace except this one corroded machine. (type: central)
- The ironic counterpoint: the device may have been prone to jamming. This does not diminish it — it reframes it as a monument to understanding rather than a practical tool. The builders encoded everything they knew about the sky into bronze, even if the bronze could not perfectly perform. This deepens the emotional resonance of the central reveal. (type: reframing)
