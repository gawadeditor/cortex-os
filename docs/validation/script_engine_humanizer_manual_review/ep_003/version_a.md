# EP 003 — Version A

## Hook

In 1901, sponge divers working off the Greek island of Antikythera pulled something from a Roman shipwreck that had no business being there. A lump of corroded bronze, roughly the size of a shoebox, packed with interlocking gears so fine they would not look out of place in an eighteenth-century clock. The object was roughly two thousand years old.

## Sections

### Hook — *An Object With No Origin*

- **[hook]** In 1901, sponge divers working off the Greek island of Antikythera pulled something from a Roman shipwreck that had no business being there. A lump of corroded bronze, roughly the size of a shoebox, packed with interlocking gears so fine they would not look out of place in an eighteenth-century clock. The object was roughly two thousand years old.
- **[narration]** Archaeologists have spent more than a century studying it. They have scanned it with X-rays, mapped its inscriptions, and built working replicas. What they have not found is anything like it anywhere else in the ancient world. No earlier version. No simpler prototype. No workshop that might have made it. The most complex mechanical device from antiquity appeared, fully formed, out of nowhere.
- **[narration]** The builder left no name. No ancient source credits anyone with making it. No school, no city, no tradition claims it. For a device this sophisticated, the silence around its origin is almost as strange as the device itself.
- **[narration]** The question that has driven researchers for decades is not just what it does. It is how it could exist at all, and nothing like it would exist again for over a thousand years. Who taught the Greeks to build this?

### Context — *82 Fragments, One Ship*

- **[narration]** What survives today is 82 fragments of corroded bronze. Researchers estimate that represents roughly one-third of the original device. The rest is gone, dissolved by two thousand years on the seafloor, or simply never recovered from the wreck site.
- **[narration]** The inscriptions covering the fragments are written entirely in Koine Greek, the common dialect of the Hellenistic world. There is nothing exotic about the language. The dials encode the four-year Olympiad cycle used to track the Greek athletic festivals, and at least one calendar system tied to civic religious observance. This is not a mysterious foreign object. It is deeply, specifically Greek.
- **[narration]** That grounding matters, because it rules out the easier explanations. This was not imported from some unknown civilisation. It was made by people who organised their lives around the Olympic Games and the festival calendar. The mystery is not where it came from geographically. The mystery is how the knowledge to build it existed at all.

- **[narration]** The ship carrying the mechanism sank sometime between 70 and 60 BCE, based on the other cargo recovered from the wreck. The device was in transit, being taken somewhere by sea. We do not know where it was going, who had commissioned the journey, or whether it was a gift, a purchase, or something taken by force. Someone was carrying this object across the Mediterranean when the ship went down, and they took that knowledge with them.

### Rising Mystery — *The Brain Predates the Body*

- **[narration]** To understand why the mechanism is so difficult to explain, you need to understand what it actually does. At its core, it is an eclipse predictor. It encodes two astronomical cycles: the Saros cycle, which repeats every 18 years and allows prediction of solar and lunar eclipses, and the Metonic cycle, which reconciles the lunar and solar calendars over 19 years. Computing either of these cycles accurately required centuries of continuous, systematic sky observation. Not decades. Centuries.
- **[narration]** That observation did not happen in Greece. The Greeks were brilliant geometers, but the long patient record of nightly sky-watching that produced the Saros and Metonic data came from Babylon. Babylonian astronomers had been tracking eclipses and planetary positions for hundreds of years before any Greek geometer thought to turn that data into a physical model. The knowledge inside the mechanism is, in a real sense, not entirely Greek.
- **[reveal]** Then researchers found something that sharpened the problem considerably. The eclipse prediction dial does not use a generic astronomical scheme. It anchors to a specific starting date: 12 May 205 BCE. The physical device was almost built around 100 BCE, give or take a few decades. That means the astronomical data encoded in the mechanism was computed by someone roughly a century before the mechanism itself was constructed.
- **[narration]** The device's brain is older than its body. Whoever built the mechanism did not derive the eclipse scheme themselves, they inherited it. They took a set of astronomical calculations that already existed, already worked, and encoded them into bronze gears. The builder and the astronomer were not the same person. They may not even have been the same generation.
- **[narration]** This splits the mystery in two. There is the question of who built the device. And there is now a separate, older question: who computed the data that went into it, and how did centuries of Babylonian sky-watching end up inside a Greek machine?

### Escalation — *Hipparchus, Cicero, and the Lost School*

- **[reveal]** The answer to the second question is documented, even if the details are incomplete. Greek astronomers in the Hellenistic period absorbed Babylonian arithmetical period relations, the numerical ratios that describe how celestial bodies repeat their positions, and reworked them into geometric models. This was not a single event. It was a transfer of knowledge that unfolded across generations, as Greek scholars gained access to Babylonian records and translated observational data into the language of geometry. The mechanism is the physical product of that synthesis.
- **[narration]** But knowing the general story of Babylonian-Greek knowledge transfer still leaves the specific device unexplained. The gearing is not just sophisticated in a general sense. It is sophisticated in a particular way, a way that points toward a specific person.
- **[narration]** Hipparchus was a Greek astronomer working around 150 to 125 BCE, roughly contemporary with the likely construction of the mechanism. He developed a detailed mathematical model of the moon's motion, accounting for the fact that the moon does not move at a constant speed across the sky. His model used what geometers call an epicyclic theory, a small circle whose centre moves along a larger circle, to describe the lunar irregularity.
- **[reveal]** The lunar gearing inside the Antikythera Mechanism matches Hipparchus's epicyclic model with a precision that is not coincidental. The device does not just predict eclipses in a rough sense. It mechanises one specific astronomer's theory of how the moon moves. Hipparchus's intellectual work is physically present in the gears. The anonymous machine suddenly has a mind behind part of it.
- **[narration]** So by the time the mechanism was built, there was already a tradition: Babylonian observational data, reworked by Greek geometers, refined by Hipparchus into a precise lunar model, and then, somehow, translated into bronze gearing by a builder whose name we do not have. That is at least three generations of accumulated knowledge before a single gear was cut.
- **[narration]** And then there is Cicero. Writing in the first century BCE, Cicero described mechanical celestial models he had seen, devices that reproduced the motions of the sun, moon, and planets. He attributed them to Archimedes, who had died in 212 BCE. Cicero wrote about these objects as known things, not legends. He had seen them demonstrated. He described them in the same matter-of-fact tone he used for other objects of craft and learning.
- **[reveal]** That testimony changes the picture entirely. The Antikythera Mechanism was not unique. Similar devices existed, were demonstrated in educated Roman circles, and were attributed to one of the most famous engineers in antiquity. There was a tradition of building these machines. And every single one of them, except the object pulled from the Antikythera wreck, is gone.
- **[cta]** The question that keeps researchers returning to this evidence is one that has no clean answer: if Archimedes built similar devices a century before the mechanism was made, and if Cicero saw them demonstrated in Rome, then there was an entire school of builders working across generations, so where did all the other machines go? ### Revelation — *End of a 150-Year Line*

- **[narration]** Cicero's testimony points toward an answer that reframes the entire episode. If Archimedes was building mechanical celestial models in 3rd-century BCE Syracuse, then the tradition of geared astronomical devices did not begin with the Antikythera Mechanism. It began at least 150 years earlier.
- **[reveal]** Researcher Tony Freeth has argued that Archimedes probably founded this tradition, that the line of device-builders runs from Syracuse in the 3rd century BCE, through the Hellenistic world, absorbing Babylonian data and Hipparchan lunar theory along the way, and ends with the object recovered from the Antikythera wreck. The mechanism is not a miracle of sudden invention. It is the last surviving product of a lineage that ran for at least 150 years. The question was never how the Greeks could build something this complex out of nowhere. They did not build it out of nowhere. They built it at the end of a long road, and we simply lost the road.
- **[narration]** That reframing answers the central question of this episode. Who taught the Greeks to build this? Centuries of Babylonian observers taught them the sky. Generations of Greek geometers taught them to model it. Hipparchus taught them to describe the moon's irregularity with precision. And a school of device-builders, probably founded by Archimedes, taught them to put all of that into gears. The mechanism is not the beginning of something. It is the end.
- **[narration]** There is an ironic counterpoint to this picture. Recent engineering simulations, including work by researchers Aris Tsaravopoulos and others examining the mechanical tolerances of the surviving gears, have suggested the device may have been prone to jamming under normal operation. The gear trains are so tightly packed, and the tolerances so fine, that the mechanism may not have run smoothly as a practical instrument.
- **[reveal]** Some researchers have proposed, on this basis, that the mechanism was a demonstration model rather than a working tool, something built to show what was known about the sky, not to be consulted daily for eclipse predictions. If that reading is right, it does not diminish the device. It deepens it. The builders encoded everything they understood about the motions of the sun, moon, and planets into bronze, with full knowledge that the bronze might not perform perfectly. They were not building a clock. They were building a monument to understanding.
- **[narration]** A monument built at the end of a tradition, by people who may have known the tradition was ending. That is a different kind of object than the one most people imagine when they hear about ancient Greek engineering.

### Resolution — *What the Sea Kept*

- **[narration]** After the Hellenistic period, the tradition of geared astronomical devices disappeared. Not declined, disappeared. Nothing comparable appears in the archaeological or written record for over a thousand years, until medieval Islamic and European clockmakers began constructing astronomical instruments in the 13th and 14th centuries. Whatever school produced the Antikythera Mechanism left no students, no manuals, no successor workshops. The knowledge of how to build these devices died with the people who held it.
- **[narration]** The mechanism itself survived only by accident. It was on a ship. The ship sank. The bronze settled into the seafloor sediment and was preserved by the cold and the dark for two thousand years until a sponge diver's rope snagged on the wreck. Had the ship reached its destination, the device would almost have been melted down, worn out, or simply lost to time above the waterline like everything else from that tradition.
- **[narration]** We still do not know where the ship was going. The cargo suggests a Roman destination, possibly Rome itself, but the mechanism's origin is harder to pin down. Some researchers have noted that the calendar dials may reflect a Corinthian calendar system, which would point toward a specific city of manufacture, but that hypothesis remains contested. Others have looked at the geographic dialect implied by the Saros cycle encoding and asked whether it points to a workshop distinct from the obvious candidates of Rhodes or Alexandria. Neither question has a settled answer.
- **[narration]** What we are left with is this: an anonymous builder, working within a tradition founded perhaps by Archimedes, using astronomical data computed by someone else a century earlier, drawing on observations made by Babylonian astronomers across hundreds of years before that, produced a device that encoded the motions of the sky in bronze. Then the device was loaded onto a ship, the ship sank, and the tradition that produced it vanished from history.
- **[resolution]** The Antikythera Mechanism is not the beginning of ancient technology. It is the last physical trace of an entire way of knowing the sky. The builder is anonymous. The school is gone. The ship's destination is unknown. And somewhere on the seafloor, there may be other wrecks, other cargoes, other corroded lumps of bronze that no one has yet thought to look at carefully. The mechanism survived because the sea kept it. The question is what else the sea is keeping.
