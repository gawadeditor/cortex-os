# EP 003 — Version B

## Hook

In 1901, sponge divers working off the Greek island of Antikythera pulled something from a Roman-era shipwreck that had no business being there. A lump of corroded bronze, roughly the size of a shoebox, packed with interlocking gears so precisely cut that engineers who examined it a century later could not explain how anyone had made them. The device calculated the movements of the sun and moon, predicted eclipses decades in advance, and tracked the cycles of the Olympic Games. It was, by any measure, the most sophisticated mechanical device recovered from the ancient world.

## Sections

### Hook — *An Object That Shouldn't Exist*

- **[hook]** In 1901, sponge divers working off the Greek island of Antikythera pulled something from a Roman-era shipwreck that had no business being there. A lump of corroded bronze, roughly the size of a shoebox, packed with interlocking gears so precisely cut that engineers who examined it a century later could not explain how anyone had made them. The device calculated the movements of the sun and moon, predicted eclipses decades in advance, and tracked the cycles of the Olympic Games. It was, by any measure, the most sophisticated mechanical device recovered from the ancient world.
- **[narration]** The problem is not what it does. The problem is that nothing like it existed before it, and nothing like it would exist again for over a thousand years. Archaeologists have excavated every major site from the Hellenistic Mediterranean. They have found pottery, weapons, coins, sculpture, and the ruins of libraries. They have not found a single gear train, a single astronomical dial, a single mechanical precursor to this object. It appeared, fully formed, from a tradition that left no other physical trace.
- **[narration]** Decades of study have not produced a name. The builder is completely anonymous. The inscriptions on the device are in Koine Greek, the common tongue of the Hellenistic world, but they name no maker, no workshop, no city of origin. Whoever built this machine either chose not to sign it or belonged to a tradition where signing was not the custom. Either way, the person who understood the sky well enough to encode it in bronze has vanished entirely from the record.
- **[narration]** The question this episode is built around is not whether the device is real or how it works. Those questions have been answered. The question is how it was possible at all. Who taught the Greeks to build something like this, and why does the tradition that produced it leave no trace except one corroded machine at the bottom of the Aegean?

### Context — *82 Fragments, One Ship*

- **[narration]** What survives of the mechanism today is 82 numbered bronze fragments, held at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Together they represent roughly one-third of the original device. The rest dissolved into the sea over two thousand years, or broke apart when the ship went down, or was simply never recovered from the wreck site. What we have is a ruin. A significant ruin, but a ruin.
- **[narration]** The surviving fragments contain at least 30 interlocking bronze gears, several inscribed dials, and a back cover text that researchers have called an instruction manual. The inscriptions are entirely in Koine Greek, the administrative and literary language of the Hellenistic world from roughly 300 BCE onward. There is nothing exotic about the language. This is not a device from a lost civilisation or an unknown culture. It is firmly, unmistakably Greek.
- **[narration]** The dials encode a specific set of civic and astronomical cycles. One tracks the four-year Olympiad cycle, the calendar that governed the great Panhellenic games. Others track the Metonic cycle of 19 years, the Callippic cycle of 76 years, and the Saros cycle used to predict eclipses. These are not abstract mathematical curiosities. The Olympiad cycle in particular tells us something about the intended audience: this device was built for someone who lived inside Greek civic culture, who cared when the games were held.
- **[narration]** The shipwreck that preserved it has been dated to between 70 and 60 BCE, based on the cargo recovered alongside the mechanism: Roman-era amphorae, bronze statuary, glassware. The ship was carrying a mixed cargo of luxury goods, and the mechanism was among them. Someone was transporting this device by sea when the ship went down. We do not know where it was going, who had commissioned the journey, or whether the mechanism was a gift, a purchase, or something taken by force. The sea kept that answer.

### Rising Mystery — *The Brain Predates the Body*

- **[narration]** To understand why the mechanism is so difficult to explain, you need to understand what the knowledge inside it actually required. The Saros cycle, which the device uses to predict eclipses, is a period of approximately 18 years and 11 days after which the geometry of the sun, moon, and Earth repeats closely enough to produce similar eclipses. Identifying this cycle required continuous, systematic observation of the sky across multiple human generations. Not one astronomer's lifetime. Generations.
- **[narration]** The Metonic cycle, also encoded in the device, is a 19-year period after which the phases of the moon fall on the same days of the solar year. Meton of Athens identified this cycle in the 5th century BCE, but the observational data that confirmed it stretched back centuries before him. The knowledge inside the Antikythera Mechanism is not the product of one mind or one generation. It is the accumulated output of an observational tradition that ran for hundreds of years before anyone thought to put it in a machine.
- **[reveal]** Here is where the timeline becomes strange. The eclipse prediction dial on the mechanism contains a specific astronomical anchor: a solar eclipse dated to 12 May 205 BCE. This date is not incidental. Researchers including Tony Freeth and Alexander Jones have argued that this anchor point was used to calibrate the entire eclipse prediction scheme, which means the astronomical data encoded in the device was computed no earlier than 205 BCE. The physical device, however, was almost certainly built later, in the late 2nd or early 1st century BCE.
- **[narration]** The gap between those two dates is at minimum several decades. The device's astronomical scheme was worked out by someone, somewhere, before the device was physically constructed. The builder of the mechanism was not the person who computed the eclipse data. They were encoding someone else's work. The brain of this machine is older than its body, and the person who did the original computation is even further removed from us than the anonymous builder.

### Escalation — *Babylon, Hipparchus, Cicero*

- **[reveal]** The question of how Babylonian astronomical knowledge reached Greek engineers has a documented answer, even if the details are incomplete. Babylonian astronomers had been recording celestial observations on clay tablets since at least the 8th century BCE. By the 4th and 3rd centuries BCE, following Alexander's conquest of Persia, Greek scholars gained access to Babylonian astronomical archives. What they found there were centuries of precise numerical records: eclipse timings, planetary periods, arithmetical schemes for predicting lunar motion. Greek astronomers absorbed these records and then did something the Babylonians had not: they tried to build geometric models that could explain why the numbers worked.
- **[narration]** This was not a simple translation. Babylonian astronomy was fundamentally arithmetical — it produced accurate predictions through numerical patterns without asking what physical mechanism produced those patterns. Greek astronomy was fundamentally geometrical — it wanted to know the shape of the cosmos, the actual paths of the planets, the physical reason behind the numbers. The synthesis of these two traditions, Babylonian precision and Greek geometry, is the intellectual foundation on which the Antikythera Mechanism rests.
- **[reveal]** The lunar gearing inside the mechanism is where this synthesis becomes most visible. The device uses an epicyclic gear arrangement to model the varying speed of the moon across its orbit, a variation caused by the moon's elliptical path. This is not a simple approximation. The gear ratios match, with considerable precision, the lunar theory developed by the astronomer Hipparchus of Nicaea, who worked between roughly 150 and 125 BCE. Hipparchus built his lunar model partly from Babylonian period relations and partly from his own observations. Someone took that model and cut it into bronze.
- **[narration]** Hipparchus is one of the few Greek astronomers whose work survives in enough detail to compare against the mechanism's gearing. The match is not perfect, and researchers debate the exact relationship, but the structural similarity is hard to dismiss. The anonymous builder was working with ideas that trace directly to a specific person's theory of the moon. The machine is not just Greek. It is, in part, the physical expression of one astronomer's mind.
- **[reveal]** Then there is Cicero. Writing in the 1st century BCE, the Roman orator and philosopher described mechanical celestial models he had seen or heard described, and he attributed them to Archimedes of Syracuse, who died in 212 BCE. Cicero wrote about these devices not as legends or rumours but as known objects, things that had been demonstrated and discussed. He described a sphere that showed the movements of the sun, moon, and planets, and he treated it as a remarkable but real achievement of Greek craftsmanship.
- **[narration]** If Cicero is accurate, then the Antikythera Mechanism was not unique. Similar devices existed at least a century before the mechanism was built, and they were attributed to the most famous mathematician and engineer of the ancient world. The mechanism was not a one-off. It was the last known survivor of a tradition that had been producing these machines for generations. Which raises the question that sits at the centre of this episode: if there were others, where did they all go?

### Revelation — *The Last of a Dying Line*

- **[narration]** The question of who started this tradition has one serious candidate. Tony Freeth, the mathematician and filmmaker who has led some of the most detailed modern analyses of the mechanism, has argued that Archimedes probably founded the practice of building geared astronomical devices in 3rd-century BCE Syracuse. Archimedes died in 212 BCE when the city fell to Rome. His workshop, his instruments, and whatever devices he had built were either destroyed or dispersed. The tradition he started did not die with him, but it lost its most visible practitioner.
- **[reveal]** This reframes the mechanism entirely. It is not a sudden invention. It is not a miracle of isolated genius. It is the last surviving product of a tradition that ran for at least 150 years, from Archimedes in the 3rd century BCE to the anonymous builder of the late 2nd or early 1st century BCE. The central question of this episode — how could the Greeks build something this complex when nothing like it existed before — rests on a false premise. Things like it did exist before. We simply have none of them. The mechanism survived because it drowned.
- **[narration]** The answer to who taught the Greeks the sky is not a single name. It is a chain. Babylonian observers recorded the sky for centuries and built the numerical foundation. Greek geometers absorbed those records and built theoretical models. Hipparchus refined the lunar theory. Archimedes, or someone in his generation, first put gears to the problem. And then a builder whose name we do not have encoded all of it into the device that ended up on a ship, in a storm, at the bottom of the Aegean.
- **[narration]** There is one more thing worth saying about the mechanism, and it complicates the picture in an interesting way. Recent engineering work, including simulations by researchers Aris Tsaravopoulos and others, has suggested that the device may have been prone to jamming. The gear trains, while conceptually elegant, may not have run smoothly in practice. Some researchers have proposed that the mechanism was a demonstration model rather than a working instrument used for daily calculation.
- **[reveal]** If that is true, it does not diminish the device. It reframes it. The builders encoded everything they understood about the sky into bronze, with extraordinary precision, even if the bronze could not perfectly perform what the mathematics described. The mechanism was not a tool for navigating ships or scheduling harvests. It was a monument to understanding. It said: this is what we know about the sky, and here is the proof, made physical. The imperfection of the gears does not contradict that. It confirms it.

### Resolution — *What the Tradition Left Behind*

- **[resolution]** The tradition of geared astronomical devices did not fade gradually. It stopped. After the Hellenistic period, no comparable mechanism appears in the archaeological record for over a thousand years. The next devices of similar conceptual complexity are the Islamic astronomical instruments of the medieval period and, later, the mechanical clocks of 14th-century Europe. Whatever knowledge sustained the Hellenistic tradition of device-building, it was not passed on in any form that left a physical trace.
- **[resolution]** The mechanism itself was being carried somewhere when it was lost. The ship's cargo suggests a wealthy Roman-era owner, possibly someone transporting luxury goods and intellectual prestige objects from the Greek east toward Italy. Some researchers have noted that the calendar dials may use a Corinthian month system, which would point toward a northwestern Greek origin rather than Rhodes or Alexandria, the two cities most often proposed as the device's birthplace. The Saros eclipse dialect encoded in the back cover text has also been examined for geographic clues, though no consensus has emerged. The destination of the ship, the identity of the owner, and the reason for the journey remain open.
- **[resolution]** The builder is anonymous. The school that trained the builder left no workshop, no student records, no other surviving device. The ship that carried the mechanism went down in a storm or on rocks, and the mechanism spent roughly two thousand years on the seafloor before a diver's rope snagged it. Its survival was an accident of catastrophe. If the ship had reached its destination, the mechanism might have sat in a Roman villa, been melted down for bronze, or simply worn out and been discarded. We would never have known it existed.
- **[resolution]** What the mechanism represents, in the end, is not a mystery about technology. It is a mystery about transmission. How does a civilisation's deepest knowledge survive? Not always in libraries, not always in texts, and apparently not always in the machines themselves. The Babylonian-Greek synthesis that produced this device was carried in minds, passed between teachers and students, and when the last of those students died without passing it on, the tradition ended. The mechanism is the only physical object that proves it ever existed. It is not the beginning of something. It is the last breath of something that was already dying when the ship went down.
