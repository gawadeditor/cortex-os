## Sections

### Hook — *A Rock With Gears Inside*

- **[hook]** In 1900, a sponge diver named Elias Stadiatis dropped into the water off a small Greek island called Antikythera and came back up talking about a heap of rotting horses on the seafloor. He meant statues. Bronze and marble figures scattered across a Roman cargo ship that had been sitting there for two thousand years. The divers went back down, and over the following months they pulled up some of the finest ancient sculpture ever recovered from the sea.
- **[narration]** Among the statues and the amphorae and the corroded bronze, there was a lump. Roughly the size of a large dictionary. Encrusted with two millennia of calcium and sea growth, it looked like a rock. Nobody paid it much attention at first. Then it split open on the museum floor in Athens, and the people standing nearby saw something that had no business being there: gears.
- **[narration]** Not crude gears. Precisely cut, interlocking bronze wheels with triangular teeth, arranged in a configuration that suggested a machine of real sophistication. The question that followed was simple to ask and has taken over a century to partially answer: who built this, and how did they know what they knew?

### Context — *The World It Came From*

- **[narration]** The ship went down sometime around 60 BCE, give or take a decade. It was a large Roman cargo vessel, probably traveling from the eastern Mediterranean toward Italy. The Antikythera island sits in the channel between Crete and the Greek mainland, a stretch of water that has wrecked ships across every era of recorded history. This one hit the rocks and settled at around 45 meters, deep enough to preserve its cargo from casual salvage but shallow enough for the sponge divers to reach.
- **[narration]** The world the ship came from was the late Hellenistic Mediterranean. Greek science had been accumulating for three centuries by then. Euclid had systematized geometry. Eratosthenes had calculated the circumference of the Earth with reasonable accuracy. Hipparchus of Nicaea had spent decades cataloguing stars and working out the mathematics of the Moon's irregular orbit. This was not a world ignorant of precision. But it was a world that, as far as anyone knew, had not yet built machines.
- **[narration]** The mechanism sat in the Athens museum largely ignored for decades. A few researchers noted it, wrote brief descriptions, moved on. The tools to understand what it actually was did not yet exist. It took until the 1950s for a British science historian named Derek de Solla Price to look at it seriously, and what he found when he did changed the question entirely.

### Rising Mystery — *Thirty Gears, One Crank*

- **[narration]** Price spent years studying the fragments. He used early X-ray imaging to see through the encrustation and count the gear teeth without destroying what remained. What he found was not a simple device. The mechanism had at least thirty gears, possibly more, arranged across multiple layers and driven by a single hand crank on the side. Turning that crank moved all the dials simultaneously.
- **[narration]** The dials tracked the Sun and Moon through the zodiac. One display showed the phase of the Moon on any given date. Another tracked the Metonic cycle, a 19-year period after which the Sun and Moon return to roughly the same positions relative to each other. The Metonic cycle was known to Greek astronomers, but encoding it mechanically required a gear ratio of 254 teeth to 19, a ratio that appears in the mechanism with precision that Price described as extraordinary.
- **[narration]** Price published his analysis in 1959 and again in a longer study in 1974. His conclusion was that the mechanism was an analog computer, a device for calculating astronomical positions by turning a physical model of the cosmos. The engineering community received this with something between fascination and disbelief. Nothing remotely comparable had been found anywhere in the ancient world. The next known mechanical clock of similar complexity would not appear for another fourteen hundred years.

### Escalation — *What the Scans Found*

- **[narration]** In 2005, a research consortium called the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project brought the fragments to a company in England that builds industrial CT scanners for aerospace. The machine they used was large enough to scan aircraft engine components. Over several days, it produced three-dimensional X-ray images of the mechanism's interior at a resolution of about a tenth of a millimeter. For the first time, researchers could read the inscriptions on the internal plates and count every gear tooth without touching the object.
- **[narration]** What the scans revealed pushed the complexity further than Price had estimated. The back of the mechanism carried two large spiral dials. The upper one tracked the Saros cycle, an 18-year period used to predict eclipses. The lower one tracked the Exeligmos, a triple Saros of 54 years that allowed eclipse predictions to be corrected for time of day. The mechanism did not just display the sky. It predicted specific astronomical events, months and years in advance, with enough accuracy to be practically useful.
- **[narration]** The inscriptions added another layer. The text on the back cover described the mechanism's functions in plain language, almost like a user manual. Researchers found references to the planets, to the colors of eclipses, and to a four-year cycle that corresponded to the Panhellenic games, including the Olympics. The device was not purely a scientific instrument. It was also a calendar of civic and religious life, something a person would consult to know when the games were coming, when to expect an eclipse, where the planets would stand.
- **[narration]** A 2016 study added the planets themselves. Researchers at University College London proposed a reconstruction in which the front of the mechanism carried additional gearing to display the positions of all five planets known to antiquity: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn. The gear trains required to do this are complex enough that the full mechanism, if the reconstruction is correct, modeled the entire observable solar system as understood by Greek astronomy. The question of who designed something like that became harder to answer, not easier.

### Revelation — *Rhodes and Hipparchus*

- **[reveal]** The strongest candidate is Hipparchus of Nicaea, or someone working directly from his calculations. Hipparchus was active on the island of Rhodes between roughly 190 and 120 BCE, about a century before the ship went down. He is the astronomer who worked out the precise irregularity of the Moon's orbit, a problem that requires exactly the kind of mathematics encoded in the mechanism's lunar gearing. The gear ratio for the Moon's anomaly in the mechanism matches Hipparchus's own published figures.
- **[narration]** Rhodes matters for another reason. The mechanism's eclipse predictions are calibrated to a geographic latitude that corresponds to Rhodes, not to Alexandria or Athens. The Saros series encoded in the back dials begins with an eclipse that occurred in 205 BCE, a date consistent with Rhodian astronomical records. The dial for the Panhellenic games includes a competition called the Halieia, a festival held specifically at Rhodes. The device was almost certainly made there.
- **[narration]** This does not mean Hipparchus built it with his own hands. It means the mechanism was built by someone who had access to his work, probably a craftsman or a small workshop operating within the tradition he established. Ancient sources mention that Rhodes had a reputation for mechanical ingenuity. Cicero, writing around the time the ship sank, described a device made by Posidonius of Rhodes that showed the movements of the Sun, Moon, and planets. He may have been describing something very close to what the divers pulled up two thousand years later.

### Resolution — *What We Still Don't Know*

- **[narration]** What we have is a fragment. Literally: the mechanism survives in 82 pieces, and researchers estimate those pieces represent about a third of the original device. The rest is still somewhere on the seafloor off Antikythera, or dissolved into the sea centuries ago. What we can reconstruct from what remains is already more than most people thought the ancient world was capable of. What the complete device looked like, and what else it could do, is a question the evidence cannot yet answer.
- **[narration]** The deeper problem is that the mechanism appears to be alone. No other device of comparable complexity has been found from antiquity. Either it was genuinely rare, a one-off achievement that was never widely replicated, or similar devices existed and simply have not survived. Bronze is valuable. Most ancient bronze was melted down and reused long before anyone thought to preserve it. The Antikythera mechanism survived because it sank.
- **[resolution]** What the mechanism tells us, more than anything else, is that the gap between ancient capability and modern assumption is wider than we tend to think. Someone on Rhodes, working from mathematics that was already a century old, built a machine that modeled the sky well enough to predict eclipses and track the planets across decades. They did it in bronze, with hand tools, without a single written instruction that has survived. We know roughly who. We know roughly where. We still do not fully know how.
